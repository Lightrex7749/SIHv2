[SECTION: Foreword — Flood Damage Trends]
India is highly vulnerable to floods and out of the total geographical area of 329 million hectares, more than 40 million hectares is flood prone. Floods are a recurrent phenomenon, which cause huge loss of lives and damage to livelihood systems, property, infrastructure and public utilities. The average annual flood damage during 1996-2005 was Rs. 4,745 crore, compared to Rs. 1,805 crore, the corresponding average for the preceding 53 years — an increase attributed to rapid population growth, urbanisation, and growing developmental and economic activity in flood plains, alongside global warming.

[SECTION: Vulnerability to Floods]
Floods have been a recurrent phenomenon in India and cause huge losses to lives, properties, livelihood systems, infrastructure and public utilities. On an average every year, 75 lakh hectares of land is affected, about 1,600 lives are lost, and the damage caused to crops, houses and public utilities is around Rs. 1,805 crore due to floods. The maximum number of lives (11,316) were lost in the year 1977. The frequency of major floods is more than once in five years, and floods have also occurred in areas which were earlier not considered flood-prone.

[SECTION: Urban Flooding]
Flooding in cities and towns is caused by increasing incidence of heavy rainfall in a short period of time, indiscriminate encroachment of waterways, inadequate capacity of drains and lack of maintenance of drainage infrastructure. NDMA has recognised urban flooding as an issue meriting exclusive attention, with separate guidelines for its management prepared and issued.

[SECTION: Objectives of the Flood Management Guidelines]
The Guidelines rest on objectives including: shifting the focus to preparedness through a time-bound, techno-economically viable, socially acceptable and eco-friendly combination of structural and non-structural flood management measures; ensuring regular monitoring of the effectiveness and sustainability of flood-control structures; continuous modernisation of flood forecasting, early warning and decision-support systems; ensuring flood-resistant features in new structures in flood-prone areas; drawing up time-bound flood-proofing plans for strategic and public utility structures; improving awareness and preparedness of stakeholders; introducing capacity-development interventions; improving the compliance regime; and strengthening emergency response capabilities.

[SECTION: Institutional Framework — National Disaster Management Authority]
Under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, the NDMA, as the apex body, is mandated to lay down policies, plans and guidelines for disaster management to ensure timely and effective response to disasters. Its statutory functions include laying down policies on disaster management, approving the national plan and plans of central ministries and departments, laying down guidelines to be followed by state authorities in drawing up state plans, coordinating enforcement and implementation of policy, recommending funds for mitigation, and exercising general superintendence of the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF).

[SECTION: Flood Plain Zoning and Regulation]
Unplanned and unregulated developmental activities in flood plains and encroachments into waterways have led to increases in both flood losses and flood risk. Flood plain zoning regulations, based on a model bill circulated by the Central Water Commission (CWC) since 1975, have not been enacted and enforced by most states; only Rajasthan and Manipur have passed such legislation, and even in those cases steps for enforcement have not been taken.

[SECTION: Gaps in Flood Management]
Identified gaps include: non-availability of close contour maps and digital elevation models of flood-prone areas; a flood forecasting and warning network that does not cover all flood-prone rivers and rivulets; most state governments lacking their own reliable forecasting networks; flood plain zoning regulations not enacted by most states; unsatisfactory implementation of flood-proofing measures such as raising villages and constructing flood shelters; reservoir operation often disregarding flood moderation; and lack of inflow forecasting systems for reservoirs, leading to sudden releases that cause downstream flooding.

[SECTION: Capacity Development and Flood Response]
The central and state governments are required to take steps for capacity development for effective and sustainable preventive, preparatory and mitigative measures in the pre-flood stage, and for prompt and effective response during and after floods. An effective and prompt response is important for minimising loss of life and property, with communities and NGOs playing a vital role in search, rescue and relief operations, alongside the National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) constituted under the DM Act, 2005 for specialised response.

[SECTION: Achievements in Structural Measures]
Since 1954, 33,928 km of new embankments and 38,809 km of drainage channels have been constructed in the country. In addition, 2,450 town protection works have been completed and 4,721 villages have been raised above flood levels. These works have given reasonable protection to an area of about 18.22 million hectares, though the remaining flood-prone area is yet to be protected.
