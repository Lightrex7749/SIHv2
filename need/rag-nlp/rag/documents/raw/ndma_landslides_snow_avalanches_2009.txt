[SECTION: Executive Summary — Background]
The prevention of loss to life and property due to natural calamities is being viewed very seriously by the Government of India. In the past, the main role played by the Government in the case of various disasters was confined mainly to post-disaster activities that included providing relief and organising rehabilitation. The Uttarkashi Earthquake of 1991, Killari Earthquake of 1993 and the devastating Malpa landslide along the Kailash-Mansarovar route in 1998 acted as an eye-opener for the Government. The need was felt for a proactive approach rather than waiting for a disaster to occur. The Disaster Management Act, 2005, was enacted on 23 December 2005 and the National Disaster Management Authority, a statutory body under the chairmanship of the Prime Minister, was set up. As per the Disaster Management Act, the responsibility to cope with natural disasters is essentially that of state governments and the role of the central government is a supportive one in terms of supplementing physical and financial resources.

[SECTION: Executive Summary — Landslide Risk in India]
Landslides are one of the natural hazards that affect at least 15 per cent of the land area of India — an area which exceeds 0.49 million sq. km. Landslides of different types are frequent in geodynamically active domains in the Himalayan and Arakan-Yoma belt of the North-Eastern parts of the country as well as in the relatively stable domains of the Meghalaya Plateau, Western Ghats and Nilgiri Hills. In all, 22 states and parts of the Union Territory of Puducherry and Andaman & Nicobar Islands are affected by this hazard. The phenomenon of landslides is pronounced during the monsoon period. In 2005 alone, more than 500 human lives were lost due to this hazard in the country.

[SECTION: Nine Major Areas for Landslide Hazard Management]
The Guidelines identify nine major areas for systematic and coordinated management of landslide hazards: (i) landslide hazard, vulnerability and risk assessment; (ii) multi-hazard conceptualisation; (iii) landslide remediation practice; (iv) research and development, monitoring and early warning; (v) knowledge network and management; (vi) capacity building and training; (vii) public awareness and education; (viii) emergency preparedness and response; and (ix) regulation and enforcement.

[SECTION: Landslide Hazard Zonation Mapping]
Landslide hazard and risk assessment is carried out through landslide hazard zonation (LHZ) mapping and geological and geotechnical investigation of vulnerable slopes and existing landslides. Hazard zonation mapping involves creation of a landslide inventory, selecting scales for mapping depending on end-user requirements, selecting landslide hazard zonation methodologies for different scales, multi-hazard integration (especially integrating seismic hazard), prioritisation of areas for LHZ mapping, and landslide risk zonation. Maps of 1:1,00,000 or 1:50,000 scale are considered indicative only and inappropriate for detailed local decisions; larger scale maps at 1:10,000 or 1:5,000 are used for detailed local-level studies.

[SECTION: Landslide Risk Treatment Strategy]
Landslide risk treatment is the ultimate objective of the risk management process. It comprises a five-pronged strategy: (i) treating vulnerable slopes and existing hazardous landslides; (ii) restricting development in landslide-prone areas; (iii) preparing codes for excavation, construction and grading; (iv) protecting existing developments; and (v) monitoring and warning systems, together with arrangements for landslide insurance and compensation for losses. Risk treatment of already distressed slopes includes four broad types of remediation practice: control works, restraint works, slope protection works, and mass improvement techniques.

[SECTION: Human Settlements in Landslide Prone Areas]
As the growth of urban, semi-urban and rural centres, infrastructural developments, and other developmental activities in landslide-prone areas increases, the risk of landslides rises manifold unless mitigation issues are addressed adequately. District plans for land use may be developed to minimise the risk of landslide hazard. Rules included in state plans control various aspects of development in landslide hazard-prone areas, including design, construction, location and density, and include provisions to ensure that risk does not increase due to unplanned urbanisation, intensified improper land use, or new construction in high-hazard areas.

[SECTION: Early Warning Systems for Landslides]
Early warning is a process involving three components: (i) scientific and technical communities; (ii) government authorities and civil agencies; and (iii) local communities. Involvement of local communities is crucially important — an aware and vigilant community sensitised to the warning signs of impending landslides is a vital pillar for implementation of an effective early warning system. Real-time monitoring may be undertaken for a few devastating, large-dimension and recurring slides or rock falls that are difficult to stabilise and pose a high risk.

[SECTION: Institutional Framework — Nodal Agencies]
The Geological Survey of India (GSI) was declared the nodal agency for landslides by the Government in January 2004, with responsibility for coordinating activities related to landslide hazard mitigation and monitoring landslide occurrence in the country. The Department of Science and Technology (DST) and the Ministry of Environment and Forests were identified as nodal agencies for geotechnical investigations and land-use zonation and regulation respectively, following task forces set up after the 1998 Malpa tragedy.

[SECTION: Glossary — Key Terms]
Hazard: a threatening event or the probability of occurrence of a potentially damaging phenomenon within a given time period and area. Vulnerability: the degree of loss to a given element at risk resulting from the occurrence of a natural or man-made phenomenon of a given magnitude, expressed on a scale from 0 (no damage) to 1 (total loss). Risk: the anticipated number of lives in danger, damage to property and disruption of economic activity due to a particular natural phenomenon. Landslide Hazard Zonation Map: map of spatial and temporal extent of landslide hazard indicating areas that are, or could be, affected by landslides, assessing the probability of occurrence within a specific period. Landslide Risk Map: a map that integrates landslide hazard, landslide vulnerability and quantification of elements at risk.
